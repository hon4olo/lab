# Luck, Skill and Randomness

## The fairness rule

**Randomness before a decision is interesting. Randomness after a decision is unfair.**

- Rolling to see which three upgrades are offered, then letting the player choose: fair. The randomness set up a decision.
- Rolling to see whether the player's correct input succeeds: unfair. The randomness overrode a decision.

Almost every "this game is random and I hate it" complaint traces to randomness applied after the player committed.

## Measuring the ratio

Have one strong player repeat identical content 10 times and record outcomes.

| Spread of results | Reading |
|---|---|
| Nearly identical | Skill-dominant. Correct for puzzles and precision games |
| Moderate variance, correlated with play quality | Healthy for most games |
| Wide variance, uncorrelated with play quality | Luck-dominant. Fine for a party game, fatal for a skill game |

The number itself is not the problem. **The mismatch between the number and what the game appears to promise is the problem.** A game that looks skill-based and plays lucky feels like cheating.

## Early variance matters more than late variance

New players have no baseline. Three bad rolls in the first five minutes are not read as variance — they are read as "this game is broken and unfair", and the player leaves before the distribution has a chance to even out.

Reduce variance deliberately in the first few attempts:

- guarantee a decent starting option in run 1
- suppress the harshest outcomes until the player has succeeded once
- use pity timers so a rare reward is guaranteed within N attempts

This is not condescension; it is correcting for the fact that a small sample of a fair distribution looks unfair.

## Tools that make randomness feel fair

| Tool | Effect |
|---|---|
| Preview | The player sees what is coming and can plan around it |
| Mitigation | A reroll, a shield, a save — agency over the roll |
| Pity timer | Guarantees the rare outcome within a bounded window |
| Bounded ranges | Damage 8–12 instead of 1–20 |
| Draw without replacement | Shuffled deck rather than independent rolls; prevents long droughts |
| Visible odds | Stated probabilities. Hidden odds are read as rigged, and in many places must be disclosed anyway |

## Hidden information

Distinct from randomness, and often better. Hidden information the player can deduce or scout creates strategy; randomness the player cannot influence creates variance. Where you can, replace a roll with something concealed but knowable.
